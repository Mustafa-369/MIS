1 · The big picture

Your whole business runs as four ways of fulfilling work sitting on one shared spine of core information, wrapped by services every department reuses. Standard fittings are machined on CNC; custom parts are turned by hand; hose is assembled; and some things are simply bought and resold. All of it flows through the same items, the same stock, the same orders.

The BRAIN — one spine, four ways to fulfil

The three reasons it exists. Every part of the BRAIN serves one of your three winning measures: Quality (full traceability + revision control + quality gates), Delivery & smooth communication (scheduling, live order board, internal requisitions, and approvals that never make you wait), and Good price (true cost per job + efficiency measures). If a feature serves none of these, it does not belong here.

3 · The Neurons, with examples

Twelve decisions form the thinking core. Each is stated plainly, shown with a real example, and tagged with the categories it affects.

N-01

Departments are the menu, not separate systems

Your 17 departments decide the screens and permissions people see — not 17 databases. (We just merged the two hose categories into a single HOSE department split into process-based units — the same principle in action.) Everything sits on the one shared spine.

In real life: add a department tomorrow and we add screens and permissions — nothing underneath is rebuilt.
Applies to:All categories
N-02

One item register, and clicking a SKU shows everything

Every material, fitting, hose, tool, and finished good lives in one register. Open any SKU and see its process, its assemblies, its department, and its documents at once. Each department sees only its filtered slice. Rubber vs pharma is just an attribute here, not a category. A part SKU and an assembly SKU are separate items; a part sits in unlimited assemblies through the BOM — never owned by one.

In real life: click the SS304 nut and see its drawing, its bar, its CNC route, its cost, and every order it's on. And once you configure an assembly, Save it as an item — repeat orders pick it instantly.
Applies to:CNCTurningHose — every category's items live here.
N-03

One register for customers and suppliers

A "party" can be a customer, a supplier, or both. A generic cash party covers walk-ins.

In real life: a firm you buy bar from and sell hose to is one record with two roles — not two half-records that drift apart.
Applies to:All categories
N-04

A process is the product meeting the machines

The product says what must happen (weld this end, flare this hose, thread this fitting); the stations say how and where it can happen. Each end of a hose is configured on its own; each part is sourced on its own (stock / buy / make); and add-ons compose from the same parts. So customisation is unlimited by construction.

In real life: a silicone hose with a straight TC one end and a 90° TC-to-flange (bend turned, flange welded) the other — the BRAIN composes it from primitives; no "silicone-90°-flange" catalogue entry ever had to exist.
Affects each category differently: CNC machines fittings; Turning makes custom parts; Hose routes to whichever units its operations need.
N-05

Money is for cost, not invoices

The BRAIN computes true cost in three layers (material, conversion, overhead). No invoicing, tax, or ledgers — deliberate. Detail in the cost brain.

In real life: you learn a 665 mm assembly truly costs ₹X to make, so you price with confidence.
Applies to:All categories — cost basis differs (see the matrix).
N-06

Traceability is a family tree, and the stock record can't be secretly edited

Every lot knows its parents and children. The stock ledger is append-only and hash-chained — each entry fingerprints the one before, so tampering visibly breaks the chain. Pharma requirements (cert, cleanliness, extra traceability) are triggered automatically by the hose's pharma attribute — no separate department needed.

In real life: a pharma hose fails a test two years later — trace it back to the exact PTFE lot, supplier, and receipt, and forward to every order that lot touched.
Applies to:All — strictest for pharma-flagged hose; critical for all pressure-safety hose.
N-07

Designs are versioned — old versions are never erased

Change a design or process and a new revision is created; the old one is kept, and every job records the revision it was built to.

In real life: improve the nut from a 45 mm bar (Rev 1) to a 42 mm bar (Rev 2). New orders use Rev 2; a batch already running stays on Rev 1; any piece can be traced to the exact design it was made to.
Matters most for:CNCTurning and pharma (certified revisions); hose assemblies carry versioned BOMs too.
N-08

Shared services are built once

Login, audit, workflow, attachments, notifications, barcodes, dashboards — one set, reused everywhere.

In real life: the barcode identifying a lot on the CNC floor is the same system identifying a hose lot at Ranigunj.
Applies to:All categories
N-09

Approvals never make you wait

Approvals are a trail, not a toll booth. Work flows immediately; the responsible person is notified and can review or reverse afterwards. Only safety/quality gates (first-article, pressure test) actually stop the line. Critical parts add one more hard gate — a second, Director-level authorisation on their quality check (see the callout after the Neurons).

In real life: a purchase within its limit just happens, recorded, no waiting. A pressure test genuinely blocks dispatch — because that one is safety, not paperwork.
Applies to:All categories — hard gates concentrated in hose quality.
N-10

The BRAIN knows your org chart

Department → position → employee, with reporting lines and heads. This is how the system knows who is responsible, without naming individuals in the design. A position drives its roles (WD-03), and an employee may hold several positions (roles combine). The role set: admin, sales, planner, operator, quality, procurement, viewer, plus engineer and store (there is no separate "security" role). The table is named job_position (a MySQL reserved-word rename; the concept is unchanged).

In real life: "route to the requesting department's head" always finds the right person, even after a promotion — it reads the position, not a name.
Applies to:All categories
N-11

Approval rules are settings, not code

Each rule is keyed by document type, department, and a condition (like a value threshold). Change a rule and you edit a setting — never the software.

In real life: a ₹5,000 purchase and a ₹5,00,000 purchase can follow different paths, and you can change either yourself.
Applies to:All categories
N-12

Every decision is recorded and signed — without slowing work

Actions carry who, when, and a signature; reviews can delegate and auto-escalate. The record is created while the work proceeds.

In real life: you always know who signed off what — but no operator stands idle waiting for a signature.
Applies to:All categories

⚑ Critical-part second authorisation — build into the UI

Some parts are critical / important. Because the person who designs a part may also hold quality authority, a critical part's quality gate requires a second, Director / General-Manager authorisation on top of the Quality Head before the lot can pass. It is a hard gate (N-09) driven by config (N-11). Three requirements carry into the build:

  • a critical flag on the item register (N-02);
  • an approval rule: critical part + quality gate → mandatory Director sign-off;
  • surfaced on the Quality-gate screen as a required second authorisation.

4 · Process & stations — how work really flows

A process is where the product meets the machines. The product says what must happen; the stations say how and where it can happen. Neither alone is the process — the two combined are.

A process step has a type

A process is an ordered list of steps, and each step is one of four kinds:

  • Station — runs on a machine / capability (scheduling picks the actual machine).
  • Gate — a quality checkpoint that must pass (first-article, pressure test).
  • Requirement — a manual step, no machine (packaging, finishing).
  • Outsourced — leaves to a vendor and returns (plating).

Stations bind to a capability, not a named machine

A step says "needs a TIG welding station," and scheduling picks which actual machine runs it. Unique machines (the flaring machine) resolve to the only one automatically. This keeps you flexible when a machine is busy, down, or newly bought.

Add-ons — compose anything

An add-on is one or more SKUs + an operation + a disposition (built into the assembly, or a companion that ships with it). Your camlock example: pick the camlock and flange SKUs, add "weld flange to camlock," mark it "ships with" — and it travels with the hose, costed and traced.

Save it once, reorder instantly

Configure an assembly once (hose body + each end + add-ons + its process) and Save it as an item — a recurring SKU carrying its BOM and process. A repeat order just picks it; only length and quantity are entered. Your common assemblies become a quick-pick catalogue, and because a saved item already points at a locked process, it flows straight through.

Where spares fit

"Spare" means four different things (consumables, maintenance spares, components, packaging), kept as separate categories — detailed in Inventory. The rule here: machine consumables live on the station (a blade belongs to the cutting machine, inherited by every process that cuts); product-specific tooling (a size's crimp die, a fixture, a thread-rolling die) attaches to the operation / item, and if it breaks or is overused, CN-11 charges the excess to that job.

The "Developed" stamp on a SKU

An SKU carries a validation record — a "Developed" stamp: the approved, tested prototype design file is uploaded to the item, certifying this design is proven and trusted, with who stamped it and when. Future minor revisions are still allowed (N-07), but the stamp is the mark of trust — a record that this part reached a perfect, tested design. It lives on the SKU, not before it.

5 · Inventory & procurement

Stock is tracked per item + lot + location — every part, hose, and assembly has its own balance. Everything moves through the one append-only, hash-chained ledger, so nothing is ever edited silently.

How inventory moves

The buildable view (your 12L example)

Because the nut, the ferrule, and the body are each their own SKU with their own stock, the system answers two questions at once: how many loose 12L nuts you have, and how many complete 12L straight connectors you can build right now — the smaller of body / nut / ferrule available. So searching an assembly shows its own finished stock and what's buildable from parts on hand.

Four kinds of "spare" — kept separate

The word "spare" covers four different things, and the system never lumps them:

6 · The cost brain

Cost is built in three layers, and the golden rule is that machine cost and overhead come from two separate pots, so nothing is counted twice.

How a unit cost is built

The two-pot rule (CN-02), in plain terms

There are two hourly rates. The machine rate holds only the machine's own costs (its depreciation, its power, its coolant, its own maintenance). The overhead rate holds everything else that keeps the plant open (indirect staff, rent, lighting, shared consumables). Every rupee sits in exactly one pot, so when a part is charged both rates it is never charged twice. In our worked example: a machine rate of ~₹211/hr and an overhead rate of ~₹71/hr gave a unit cost of ~₹149.80. Your real numbers replace these.

The eleven Cost Neurons (detail)
  • CN-01 — three layers: material, conversion, overhead.
  • CN-02 — the two-pot rule: machine costs and overhead never mix.
  • CN-03 — predictable scrap (per unit) is separate from stock-count losses (periodic, pooled).
  • CN-04 — recovered scrap is credited at the market salvage rate (updates over time).
  • CN-05 — inbound freight is added to the material's landed cost.
  • CN-06 — all rates are date-stamped, so old jobs keep old rates.
  • CN-07 — product-specific tooling is charged to that product; general consumables are pooled.
  • CN-08 — material = actual value; conversion = rate × time; overhead = a set rate.
  • CN-09 — every job has an estimate (for quoting) and an actual (after), with the gap shown.
  • CN-10 — material must balance: bought = produced + sold + in stock; any gap is charged to cost, not hidden.
  • CN-11 — actual tooling: a tool that breaks, or is used more than it should be, is charged extra to that job.

10 · Scope & build order

Deliberately out: invoicing, receivables/payables, ledgers, tax; payroll (attendance stays, for cost only). Seams left open: multi-site (already core), a customer-portal boundary, the full R&D workspace, and standard-cost analysis later. The confidential document vault (role-scoped, audited, encrypted) is in the foundation now.

Build & upload order — one register at a time, verified live, then the next:

  • Wave 0 — locations, departments, cost centres, work centres/units, positions, roles.
  • Wave 1 — employees, users, machines/stations (with capabilities & consumables).
  • Wave 2 — units, categories (consumable / component / packaging / …), attributes, reorder + procurement flags → item register → party register.
  • Wave 3 — BOMs, process templates (typed steps bound to stations), drawings.
  • Wave 3.5 — cost setup: bases, rates, pools, salvage, tooling.
  • Wave 4 — opening stock by item / lot / location.
  • Wave 5+ — orders, add-ons, requisitions, two-track procurement, work orders, quality, transfers, dispatch, cost runs, approvals, the confidential document vault — then remaining departments (incl. R&D workspace) as screens.

11 · How to change the BRAIN

Locking does not freeze the BRAIN — it puts it under change control. You can always add to it; the lock means changes happen deliberately and on the record, never by accident. This section is written for people and for Claude / Claude Code, so every change follows the same safe path.

Three sizes of change

SizeExamplesRiskWhat to do
Add data (99% of use)a new hose, fitting, customer, add-on, or a process built from existing stationsNone — it's just using the BRAINJust enter it. Nothing structural moves.
Add a capabilitya new item category, station capability, attribute, report, or screenLow — additive by designAdd alongside the existing; old data keeps working.
Change a Neuron / rulea new costing rule, a department restructure, a new invariantReal — can rippleThe five-step process below.

The five-step process for a Neuron or rule change

  1. Propose it in this document — what changes, and why.
  2. Trace the ripple — which registers, workflows, and other Neurons it touches.
  3. Bump the version — add a revision-history line; the old version is never erased (N-07 applied to the BRAIN itself).
  4. Migrate, don't rebuild — add tables / columns / rows; never tear down what is loaded and running.
  5. Pass the gate — the automated test suite (the reborn runAudit) must pass before the change ships.

Rules for Claude & Claude Code — must follow

  • Never change a locked Neuron without explicit human approval, recorded as a revision-history line here.
  • Update this document first, then implement — code follows the BRAIN, never the other way round.
  • Additive, never destructive — only add structures and data; never DROP or rebuild an existing table/column, or delete live data, to satisfy a change. Migrate forward.
  • Versioned everything — never overwrite a design, process, or BOM revision; create a new revision.
  • The stock ledger is immutable — never UPDATE or DELETE a stock_movement row; corrections are new, offsetting entries.
  • The test suite is the release gate — nothing commits unless runAudit passes.
